Research

Applied generative AI research in model fine-tuning, controlled image and video generation, and quantitative evaluation.

These applied research studies tackle concrete machine learning problems, from identity-preserving fine-tuning and controlled image generation to scalable clustering of large malware corpora. Each study (except clustering) follows an end-to-end workflow spanning problem formulation, experimental design, model adaptation, hyperparameter optimization, quantitative evaluation, and error analysis.

The work applies foundation models such as FLUX.1-dev using parameter-efficient fine-tuning with low-rank adaptation (LoRA) and controlled generation with ControlNet. Experiments are evaluated against explicit metrics, including face similarity and image-text similarity. Findings are grounded in systematic experiments and reproducible evaluation, with documented trade-offs, limitations, and failure modes.

Continuous-Time Generative Models

Mathematical foundations of generative modeling, covering diffusion, flow matching, stochastic differential equations, and sampling algorithms from first principles.

This series develops the mathematical foundations of modern generative AI through first-principles derivations, drawing on stochastic calculus and stochastic processes. Topics include diffusion models, score-based stochastic differential equations (SDEs), flow matching, optimal transport, Langevin sampling, and the design space of diffusion models.

The focus is on deriving training objectives, understanding the underlying probabilistic dynamics, and analyzing sampling algorithms, including accelerated generation through ordinary differential equation (ODE) solvers, distillation, and consistency models. The emphasis is on mathematical reasoning that explains why these methods work, not just how they are implemented.

I used to work in quantitative finance, but when generative AI began reshaping whole industries I grew curious enough to investigate how the new models worked. The deeper I went, the more I kept meeting old friends wearing new names. The “forward process” in a diffusion paper was an Ornstein-Uhlenbeck process used for modeling interest rates. The same paper’s “reverse process”, which generates the image, can be derived by Girsanov’s theorem [1], which gives the same \mathbb{P}\to\mathbb{Q} change of measure that sits underneath every option price. The vocabularies had diverged; the mathematics had not. What was new was the application, not the machinery: a familiar toolkit aimed at a new field.

Digging into why the two lined up so neatly, I found that none of it began in either field. It started with Robert Brown in 1827, watching particles released from pollen grains jitter under a microscope and publishing the account the next year [2]. Einstein explained the jitter in 1905 [3]; Norbert Wiener made it rigorous in the 1920s as the Wiener process [4]. Kiyosi Itô then built stochastic calculus in the 1940s [5]: a chain rule for random paths (Itô’s lemma) and integration against Brownian motion. Its analytic twins, the Fokker-Planck equation [6], [7] for how a density evolves and the Feynman-Kac formula [8] linking such partial differential equations (PDEs) to expectations, complete the framework. Both finance and generative AI are downstream of physicists and mathematicians who were thinking about neither.

Finance borrowed it early. Louis Bachelier modeled stock prices as Brownian motion in 1900 [9], five years before Einstein’s paper, though it sat ignored for decades. Real adoption came in 1973, when Black, Scholes [10], and Merton [11] priced options by modeling the stock as geometric Brownian motion and applying Itô’s lemma to a hedged portfolio; the resulting PDE solves, via Feynman-Kac, to an expected discounted payoff. From there it spread: the Ornstein-Uhlenbeck process [12] became the Vasicek model [13] of mean-reverting rates, and the Hamilton-Jacobi-Bellman equation the backbone of optimal portfolio choice [14]. A quant lives inside SDEs and PDE-expectation duality daily, rarely calling them by their physics names.

Diffusion models are borrowing it now. A generator defines a forward process that turns data into noise, then learns to run it backward. Song and colleagues’ 2021 framework [15] wrote this as an SDE; Anderson’s 1982 result [16] on reverse-time diffusions shows the generative process is that SDE run backward, driven by the score, the gradient of the log density. The most common forward choice, the Variance-Preserving SDE, is a time-dependent Ornstein-Uhlenbeck process; the marginal distribution evolves by Fokker-Planck. A quant reading this is not learning new mathematics, but recognizing familiar territory.

That is why the crossing is easy both ways. The core skill is identical: fluency with stochastic processes and Itô calculus, the instinct to move between an SDE and its PDE, and a feel for how probability flows under drift plus diffusion. A quant who knows why a martingale has no drift and how to discretize an SDE for Monte Carlo already owns the hard part of diffusion; an AI researcher who has tuned samplers and reasoned about score matching has practiced the continuous-time skills at the heart of quantitative finance. The new material on each side is real but learnable, sitting atop a shared core.

So here is my case to both rooms. The barrier is lexical, not conceptual: “risk-neutral drift” and “reverse-time drift” are two names for one operation, reshaping the drift by a change of measure, so each field keeps rediscovering what the other knows. Ideas already migrate both ways, but only the hard way, through specialists who stay in one field and learn the other’s dialect to do the work. Bühler, Teichmann and colleagues pulled the neural network, a tool born in AI, into derivatives hedging [17]; Wang, Zariphopoulou and Zhou carried stochastic optimal control, central to quantitative finance, into the AI problem of reinforcement learning [18]. That it works at all is the point: it works because the mathematics is already shared. It could happen far more readily, and by design. The wall between you is thinner than it looks, and some of the best ideas you could borrow are already sitting on the other side.

Now that you have the full context, dive in. Each post is math-first and derives its results in full, building on the posts before it. They are listed below in reading order, starting with post 1, each with a summary of what it covers.

This post lays the mathematical foundation for the entire series. It begins with Brownian motion and the key rule (dW)^2=dt, uses them to derive Itô’s lemma from first principles, and solves geometric Brownian motion and the Ornstein-Uhlenbeck process with it. The same result then derives the Black-Scholes equation through hedging and the no-arbitrage condition.

After that the post introduces generative models, explains variational autoencoders, normalizing flows, and energy-based models, and presents diffusion models, their two lineages and Denoising Diffusion Probabilistic Models (DDPM).

It then formulates DDPM’s forward process as a Variance-Preserving SDE and returns to Itô’s lemma twice, deriving its perturbation kernel and the Fokker-Planck equation. A closing comparison sets the three derivations side by side, so quantitative finance and generative AI meet through the same mathematics.

This post focuses entirely on generative AI, tracing the two main lineages of diffusion modeling. It begins with the variational route, from latent-variable models to variational autoencoders (VAEs), the evidence lower bound (ELBO), variational inference, and hierarchical VAEs. It then shows how DDPM can be understood as a hierarchical VAE with a fixed encoder, trained with the same variational bound, which reduces to a noise-prediction loss, and sampled through ancestral sampling.

The second half develops the score-based route from first principles. Energy-based models leave a normalizing constant that cannot be computed, and the score does not depend on it. Explicit and implicit score matching with Langevin sampling work in principle but meet four pitfalls on real data. Denoising score matching resolves them and cuts the cost per example from O(d) to O(1). Vincent’s theorem shows it has the same minimizer as score matching on the perturbed data, and Tweedie’s formula ties that minimizer to the posterior mean.

A single noise level still forces a dilemma. Song and Ermon’s score matching with Langevin dynamics resolves it by perturbing the data at a finite sequence of noise levels, training one noise-conditional score network across them, and sampling with annealed Langevin dynamics. The post closes by weighing what the reformulation from densities to scores bought and what it cost, leaving the unification of the two lineages to post 3.

This post brings the two lineages together in continuous time, showing that both are instances of one family of linear SDEs. It presents the Variance-Exploding (VE) and Variance-Preserving (VP) SDEs as its two variance regimes, one with no drift and growing variance, the other with mean-reverting drift and bounded variance.

Anderson’s theorem then provides the reverse-time SDE, whose one unknown term is the score and whose solution reproduces the forward process in reverse. The post derives this result twice, through the Fokker-Planck equation and through Bayes’ rule, whose reweighting is Girsanov’s change of measure. It then introduces the probability-flow ODE, whose deterministic solution has the same marginals as the solutions of the forward and reverse-time SDEs.

It shows how denoising score matching learns the score and, with DDPM’s weighting, reduces to DDPM’s noise-prediction loss. It then solves the reverse-time SDE with three samplers. Reverse diffusion sampling and post 2’s ancestral sampling are two discretizations of the same equation, and predictor-corrector sampling adds post 2’s Langevin steps as corrections.

Oct 22, 2026 Flow-Based Generative Models: When Probability Flows Like a Fluid

How the 18th-century mathematics of Euler made generative models faster to sample and simpler to train.

normalizing flows

This post asks whether the velocity field that carries noise toward data can be learned directly, by regression, without learning the score first. Diffusion models also need hundreds or thousands of network evaluations per sample, their trajectories curve and change speed, the forward SDE fixes the path of distributions between data and noise, and generation starts from Gaussian noise. The post begins with normalizing flows, which give exact likelihoods but need tractable Jacobian determinants and often deep stacks of maps. Flow models replace the maps with an ODE, drawing their mathematics from Euler’s description of fluid motion, and sample by solving it numerically, with exact likelihoods, but their maximum-likelihood training solves the ODE at each training step and leaves the path between noise and data unspecified.

Flow matching specifies a path of intermediate distributions and learns its velocity field by regression, without solving the ODE, which gives another view of post 3’s probability-flow ODE, re-derived here from the continuity equation. The continuity equation does not determine that velocity field uniquely, and the marginal velocity field, the particular one that flow matching regresses onto, is intractable, so it offers no direct regression target. Lipman et al. build the path from conditional paths, one per data point, whose velocity fields are known in closed form. Averaging them, weighted by the posterior probability of each data point given the current sample, gives a velocity field that transports the marginal distributions along the path, and the conditional and marginal objectives have the same gradient.

Gaussian conditional paths make the construction concrete. The perturbation kernels of the VP and VE SDEs, with time reversed, are instances, so diffusion is approximately a special case. The simplest of them moves each noise sample along a straight line to a data point at constant speed, with their difference as its velocity. The post ends with the limitations these paths keep: curved marginal trajectories, independent pairing of noise and data, and a Gaussian source.
