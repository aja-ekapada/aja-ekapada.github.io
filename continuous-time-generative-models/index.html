This is a working series on the continuous-time stochastic-process family that underlies modern generative AI — diffusion models, score-based SDEs, flow matching, Langevin sampling, and conditional generation — explained by someone who used the same toolkit in quantitative finance. Each topic is derived rather than described. Note: it doesn’t cover other families like autoregressive models, GANs, or masked-token methods.

I used to work in quantitative finance, but when generative AI began reshaping whole industries I grew curious enough to investigate how the new models worked. The deeper I went, the more I kept meeting old friends wearing new names. The “forward process” in a diffusion paper was an Ornstein-Uhlenbeck process used for modeling interest rates. The same paper’s “reverse process”, which generates the image, can be derived by Girsanov’s theorem [1] — the same \mathbb{P}\to\mathbb{Q} change of measure that sits underneath every option price. The vocabularies had diverged; the mathematics had not. What was new was the application, not the machinery — a familiar toolkit aimed at a new field.

Digging into why the two lined up so neatly, I found that none of it began in either field. It started with Robert Brown in 1827, watching particles released from pollen grains jitter under a microscope and publishing the account the next year [2]. Einstein explained the jitter in 1905 [3]; Norbert Wiener made it rigorous in the 1920s as the Wiener process [4]. Kiyosi Itô then built stochastic calculus in the 1940s [5]: a chain rule for random paths (Itô’s lemma) and integration against Brownian motion. Its analytic twins, the Fokker-Planck equation [6], [7] for how a density evolves and the Feynman-Kac formula [8] linking such partial differential equations (PDEs) to expectations, complete the picture. Both finance and generative AI are downstream of physicists and mathematicians who were thinking about neither.

Finance borrowed it early. Louis Bachelier modeled stock prices as Brownian motion in 1900 [9] — five years before Einstein’s paper — though it sat ignored for decades. Real adoption came in 1973, when Black, Scholes, and Merton [10], [11] priced options by modeling the stock as geometric Brownian motion and applying Itô’s lemma to a hedged portfolio; the resulting PDE solves, via Feynman-Kac, to an expected discounted payoff. From there it spread: the Ornstein-Uhlenbeck process [12] became the Vasicek model [13] of mean-reverting rates, and the Hamilton-Jacobi-Bellman equation the backbone of optimal portfolio choice [14]. A quant lives inside stochastic differential equations (SDEs) and PDE-expectation duality daily, rarely calling them by their physics names.

Diffusion models are borrowing it now. A generator defines a forward process that turns data into noise, then learns to run it backward. Song and colleagues’ 2021 framework [15] wrote this as an SDE; Anderson’s 1982 result [16] on reverse-time diffusions shows the generative process is that SDE run backward, driven by the score, the gradient of the log density. The most common forward choice, the Variance-Preserving SDE, is a time-dependent Ornstein-Uhlenbeck process; the marginal distribution evolves by Fokker-Planck. A quant reading this is not learning new mathematics, but recognizing familiar territory.

That is why the crossing is easy both ways. The core skill is identical: fluency with stochastic processes and Itô calculus, the instinct to move between an SDE and its PDE, and a feel for how probability flows under drift plus diffusion. A quant who knows why a martingale has no drift and how to discretize an SDE for Monte Carlo already owns the hard part of diffusion; an AI researcher who has tuned samplers and reasoned about score matching has practiced the continuous-time skills at the heart of quantitative finance. The new material on each side is real but learnable, sitting atop a shared core.

So here is my case to both rooms. The barrier is lexical, not conceptual: “risk-neutral drift” and “reverse-time drift” are two names for one operation, reshaping the drift by a change of measure, so each field keeps rediscovering what the other knows. Ideas already migrate both ways, but only the hard way — through specialists who stay in one field and learn the other’s dialect to do the work. Bühler, Teichmann and colleagues pulled the neural network, a tool born in AI, into derivatives hedging [17]; Wang, Zariphopoulou and Zhou carried stochastic optimal control, central to quantitative finance, into the AI problem of reinforcement learning [18]. That it works at all is the point: it works because the mathematics is already shared. It could happen far more readily, and by design. The wall between you is thinner than it looks, and some of the best ideas you could borrow are already sitting on the other side.

Post 1 builds the machinery the rest of the series runs on. It starts from Brownian motion and the single new rule (dW)^2 = dt, then develops Itô’s lemma from first principles. It turns that lemma, unchanged, on two problems half a century apart. The first is the Black-Scholes equation, reached through a hedged portfolio and no-arbitrage. The second is diffusion’s forward process, recast as the Variance-Preserving SDE with its perturbation kernel and Fokker-Planck equation. Quantitative finance and generative AI, it turns out, share an ancestor.

Post 2 crosses fully to generative AI and follows the two lineages of diffusion modeling. It takes the variational path first, through DDPM’s reverse process, the ELBO, and ancestral sampling. It then builds the score-based path from first principles, covering the score, score matching in its explicit, implicit, and denoising forms, Vincent’s theorem and Tweedie’s formula, and the noise-conditional score networks and annealed Langevin dynamics that made score-based sampling work on real data. It closes by weighing what the reformulation from densities to scores bought, and hands both lineages to post 3.

Now that you have the full context, dive in. Each post is math-first and derives its results in full, building on the posts before it. The throughline is method. At each turn the series takes a practice machine learning usually asserts, such as a loss, a noise ladder, a guidance rule, or a schedule, and derives it from the continuous-time picture until it looks less like a choice and more like a consequence. Posts are listed in reading order, first to latest.
